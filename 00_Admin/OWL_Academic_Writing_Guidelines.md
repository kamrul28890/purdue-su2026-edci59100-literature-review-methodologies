## 1. Paragraph Structure

### What is a Paragraph?
A paragraph is "a collection of related sentences dealing with a single topic." Proper paragraph construction helps writers maintain focus during drafting and revision, while enabling readers to follow ideas more easily.

**The Basic Rule:** Keep one idea to one paragraph. When transitioning to new concepts, create a separate paragraph.

---

### Four Essential Elements

#### 1. Unity
"The entire paragraph should concern itself with a single focus." A paragraph beginning with one major point must maintain that focus throughout — no topic wandering.

#### 2. Coherence
Coherence makes paragraphs understandable through logical and verbal bridges:

**Logical bridges:**
- Topic ideas carry forward between sentences
- Successive sentences use parallel construction

**Verbal bridges:**
- Repeated key words across sentences
- Synonym repetition
- Pronouns referencing previous nouns
- Transition words linking ideas

#### 3. Topic Sentence
A topic sentence indicates "what idea or thesis the paragraph is going to deal with." Position it near the beginning so readers understand the paragraph's focus before reading the evidence.

**Critical principle:** The topic sentence should state the analytical claim, not simply name the subject. The paper evidence supports the topic sentence — it does not replace it.

#### 4. Adequate Development
Topics "should be discussed fully and adequately." Paragraphs containing only two or three sentences likely lack sufficient development.

**Development methods:**
- Examples and illustrations
- Data (facts, statistics, evidence, details)
- Testimony (quotes, paraphrases)
- Term definitions
- Compare and contrast
- Cause and reason evaluation
- Effects and consequences examination
- Topic analysis or description

---

### When to Start a New Paragraph
- Beginning a new idea or point
- Contrasting information or arguments
- Readers need a pause (excessive length or complexity)
- Ending introductions or starting conclusions

---

### Transitions and Signposts
**Signposts:** Several sentences or a paragraph outlining what the article has covered and where it will be going.

**Transitions:** Usually one or several sentences that "transition" from one idea to the next, typically appearing at paragraph ends to enhance flow. They connect the argument thread — not just adjacent sentences.

## 2. Conciseness

### Core Principle
Concise writing uses "the most effective words" rather than the fewest words. Eliminate weak or unnecessary language; select strong, specific words.

---

### Strategy 1: Replace Vague Words with Powerful Ones
Use specific vocabulary instead of multiple ambiguous terms.

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| "talked about several of the merits of" | "touted" |
| "believed but could not confirm that Billy had feelings of affection" | "assumed that Billy adored" |
| "has made available many of the things you can use for making a decision" | "presents criteria for determining" |
| "Working as a pupil under someone who develops photos was an experience that really helped me learn a lot" | "Working as a photo technician's apprentice was an educational experience" |

---

### Strategy 2: Interrogate Every Word
Each word should contribute something important and unique. Delete or replace dead weight words.

| Wordy (22 words) | Concise (10 words) |
|---|---|
| "The teacher demonstrated some of the various ways and methods for cutting words from my essay that I had written for class." | "The teacher demonstrated methods for cutting words from my essay." |

---

### Strategy 3: Combine Sentences
Information that doesn't require a full sentence can be integrated into another sentence.

| Wordy (18 words) | Concise (11 words) |
|---|---|
| "Ludwig's castles are an astounding marriage of beauty and madness. By his death, he had commissioned three castles." | "Ludwig's three castles are an astounding marriage of beauty and madness." |

| Wordy (24 words) | Concise (16 words) |
|---|---|
| "The supposed crash of a UFO in Roswell, New Mexico aroused interest in extraterrestrial life. This crash is rumored to have occurred in 1947." | "The supposed 1947 crash of a UFO in Roswell, New Mexico aroused interest in extraterrestrial life." |

## 3. The Paramedic Method (7 Steps)

*Developed by Richard Lanham in Revising Prose. Reduces word count and eliminates passive voice.*

### The Seven Steps

1. Circle the **prepositions** (of, in, about, for, onto, into)
2. Draw a box around the **"is" verb forms**
3. Ask: **"Where's the action?"**
4. Change the "action" into a **simple verb**
5. Move the **doer into the subject** (Who's kicking whom?)
6. Eliminate any unnecessary **slow wind-ups**
7. Eliminate any **redundancies**

---

### Example Application

**Original (14 words):** "In this paragraph is a demonstration of the use of good style in the writing of a report."

**Revised (6 words):** "This paragraph demonstrates good style in reports."

*The action shifted from the noun "demonstration" to the verb "demonstrates."*

---

### Practice Examples with Solutions

**Example 1:**
- Original (25 words): "The point I wish to make is that the employees working at this company are in need of a much better manager of their money."
- Revised (9 words): "Employees at this company need a better money manager."

**Example 2:**
- Original (23 words): "It is widely known that the engineers at Sandia Labs have become active participants in the Search and Rescue operations in most years."
- Revised (15 words): "In recent years, engineers at Sandia Labs have participated in Search and Rescue operations."

**Example 3:**
- Original (35 words): "After reviewing the results of your previous research, and in light of the relevant information found within the context of the study, there is ample evidence for making important, significant changes to our operating procedures."
- Revised (24 words): "After reviewing the results of your research, and within the context of the study, we find evidence supporting significant changes in our operating procedures."

## 4. Reverse Paramedic Method

*Adapted from Lanham. Used for scientific writing where passive voice is appropriate.*

### Purpose
Recognizing and implementing passive voice — used in scientific writing to emphasize what was done rather than who did it.

**Active Voice:** Emphasizes the doer. "Morgan Latour attended the baseball game."
**Passive Voice:** Emphasizes what was done. "The baseball game was attended by Morgan Latour."

---

### Five-Step Procedure

1. Circle the prepositions. Eliminate any unnecessary prepositions.
2. Identify any references to the researchers (the doer).
3. Replace references to the researchers with a passive construction that emphasizes the experiment (what was done).
4. Eliminate any unnecessary slow wind-ups.
5. Eliminate any redundancies.

---

### Examples

**Example 1:**
- Original: "In the following experiment, we used the feline cell line, W9, to evaluate cell growth in the presence of the growth factor."
- Revised: "The feline cell line W9 was used to evaluate cell growth in the presence of growth factor."

**Example 2:**
- Original: "During the procedure, we cultured the cells for 48 hours in media that we modified with 78, 80, 90, and 110 ng/mL BMP."
- Revised: "Cells were cultured for 48 hours in media modified with 78, 80, 90, and 110 ng/mL BMP."

---

## 5. Active vs. Passive Voice

### Definition
**Active voice:** The subject of the sentence performs the action expressed in the verb.
- "The dog bit the boy." ✓
- "Scientists have conducted experiments to test the hypothesis." ✓

**Passive voice:** The subject receives the action.
- "Experiments were conducted by scientists to test the hypothesis."

### When to Use Each
- **Active voice:** Default for most academic writing — more direct, clearer, concise
- **Passive voice:** Appropriate in scientific/methods sections where the action (what was done) matters more than who did it; also when the doer is unknown or irrelevant

## 6. Sentence Variety

### Core Principle
"Adding sentence variety to prose can give it life and rhythm. Too many sentences with the same structure and length can grow monotonous for readers."

Longer sentences accommodate more information; shorter ones highlight important ideas.

---

### Four Sentence Types

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause, no dependent clauses | "My aunt enjoyed taking the hayride with you." |
| **Compound** | Multiple independent clauses, no dependent | "The clown frightened the little girl, and she ran off screaming." |
| **Complex** | One independent + at least one dependent clause | "After Mary added up all the sales, she discovered a shortage." |
| **Complex-Compound** | Multiple independent + at least one dependent | "*Catch-22* is widely regarded as Heller's best novel, and because Heller served in WWII, which the novel satirizes, the wit packs an extra punch." |

---

### Fixing Short, Choppy Sentences

**Technique 1 — Combine with conjunctions:** and, but, or, nor, yet, for, so
- Original: "Doonesbury cartoons satirize contemporary politics. Readers don't always find this funny. They demand that newspapers not carry the strip."
- Revised: "Doonesbury cartoons laugh at contemporary politicians, but readers don't always find this funny and demand that newspapers not carry the strip."

**Technique 2 — Subordination:** Connect sentences so one carries the main idea and the other becomes dependent.
Connectors: after, although, as, as if, because, before, even if, even though, if, rather than, since, that, though, unless, until, when, where, whereas, while

- Original: "The campus parking problem is getting worse. The university is not building any new garages."
- Revised: "The campus parking problem is getting worse because the university is not building any new garages."

---

### Fixing Repeated Subjects or Topics

**Technique 1 — Relative pronouns:** Embed one sentence within another using *which, who, whoever, whom, that, whose*
- Original: "Indiana used to be mainly an agricultural state. It has recently attracted more industry."
- Revised: "Indiana, which used to be mainly an agricultural state, has recently attracted more industry."

**Technique 2 — Participles:** Eliminate be verbs and substitute present (-ing) or past (-ed/-en) participles
- Original: "Wei Xie was surprised to get a phone call from his sister. He was happy to hear her voice again."
- Revised: "Surprised to get a phone call from his sister, Wei Xie was happy to hear her voice again."

**Technique 3 — Prepositional phrases:** Convert a sentence into a prepositional phrase
- Original: "The university has been facing pressure to cut its budget. It has eliminated funding for important programs."
- Revised: "Under pressure to cut its budget, the university has eliminated funding for important programs."

---

### Fixing Similar Sentence Patterns

**Technique 1 — Dependent markers at sentence start:**
Connectors: after, although, as, as if, because, before, even if, even though, if, in order to, since, though, unless, until, whatever, when, whenever, whether, while

- Original: "The room fell silent when the TV newscaster reported the story of the earthquake."
- Revised: "When the TV newscaster reported the story of the earthquake, the room fell silent."

**Technique 2 — Transitional words and phrases:**
accordingly, after all, afterward, also, although, and, but, consequently, despite, earlier, even though, for example, for instance, however, in conclusion, in contrast, in fact, in the meantime, in the same way, indeed, just as… so, meanwhile, moreover, nevertheless, not only… but also, now, on the contrary, on the other hand, on the whole, otherwise, regardless, shortly, similarly, specifically, still, that is, then, therefore, though, thus, yet

---

### Varying Sentence Openings
"If too many sentences start with the same word, especially *The*, *It*, *This*, or *I*, prose can grow tedious for readers."

Alternative sentence openers: prepositional phrases, dependent clauses, participial phrases, conjunctions, transitional words.

## 7. Appropriate Language

### Formality Level
"The level of formality you write with should be determined by the expectations of your audience and your purpose."

- **Formal style:** Academic essays, journal articles, cover letters — unknown or professional audience
- **Semi-formal:** Known professional colleagues
- **Informal:** Personal letters, friends — *never for academic submissions*

"Formality exists on a scale" — not rigid categories.

---

### Jargon and Technical Language
"The term 'jargon' refers to any in-group or specialized language used by small groups of like-minded individuals."

**For general audiences:** Avoid jargon without explanation. "Overloading your audience with words they do not understand will not help you achieve your purpose."

**For specialist audiences:** Use group-specific terminology. Omitting expected jargon signals non-membership or lack of mastery, damaging credibility.

**Rule:** Think about who your audience is, what they expect, and how the use of these words may help or hinder your purpose. *Define on first use; use consistently thereafter.*

---

### Avoiding Slang and Idioms in Formal Writing
Slang (y'all, cool) and idioms ("pull someone's leg," "spill the beans") reduce formality and credibility in academic writing. They also challenge non-native English speakers.

---

## 8. Adding Emphasis

### Methods
- **Italics:** Standard in published academic work. Use sparingly for genuinely critical terms.
- **Boldface:** For highlighted list items; less common in running prose.
- **Word choice and arrangement:** Preferred method in academic writing — "Academic writers are encouraged to convey emphasis through word choice and arrangement rather than relying on multiple visual effects."
- **Avoid:** ALL-CAPITALS (signals shouting), excessive font changes, size variations — "creates an impression of weak writing compensated by visual flourishes."

## 9. Establishing Arguments

### Thesis Characteristics
A thesis must be:
- **Debatable:** "The thesis must be something that people could reasonably have differing opinions on." Non-debatable claims lack persuasive purpose.
- **Narrow in scope:** "The narrower the thesis the more effective your argument will be."

Use qualifiers — "typically," "generally," "usually," "on average" — to limit scope without weakening the claim.

---

### Four Types of Claims
1. **Fact/Definition:** What something means or whether it is settled truth
2. **Cause & Effect:** One element caused another
3. **Value:** Worth, importance, or categorization of something
4. **Solutions/Policy:** Advocating for or against specific approaches

---

## 10. Logic in Argumentative Writing

### Key Vocabulary
- **Premise:** Proposition used as evidence in an argument
- **Conclusion:** Logical result of the relationship between the premises; serves as the thesis
- **Argument:** The assertion of a conclusion based on logical premises
- **Syllogism:** The simplest sequence of logical premises and conclusions (Aristotle)
- **Enthymeme:** A shortened syllogism which omits the first premise, allowing the audience to fill it in
- **Induction:** Premises provide some basis for the conclusion
- **Deduction:** Premises provide conclusive proof for the conclusion

---

### Building Logical Arguments in Writing
When converting syllogisms into written arguments:
- Lay out each premise clearly
- Provide evidence for each premise
- Draw a clear connection to the conclusion

"Arguments which could be logical are not automatically logical. They must be made logical by careful arrangement."

---

### Limitations of Logic
"Arguments must often spend as much time convincing audiences of the legitimacy of the premises as the legitimacy of the conclusions."

Audiences who operate from different foundational premises will not accept logical arguments until they adopt different values — which requires non-logical (ethos, pathos) persuasion.

## 11. Logical Fallacies

| Fallacy | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Slippery Slope** | Concludes that A will inevitably lead to Z through a chain of steps | "If we ban Hummers, eventually the government will ban all cars." |
| **Hasty Generalization** | Conclusion based on insufficient or biased evidence | "Even though it's only the first day, I can tell this is going to be a boring course." |
| **Post hoc** | Assumes that because B followed A, A caused B | "I drank bottled water and now I am sick, so the water must have made me sick." |
| **Genetic Fallacy** | Origins of an idea determine its worth | "The Volkswagen Beetle is evil because it was designed by Hitler's army." |
| **Begging the Claim** | Conclusion is embedded in the claim itself (circular) | "Filthy and polluting coal should be banned." |
| **Circular Argument** | Restates the argument rather than proving it | "George Bush is a good communicator because he speaks effectively." |
| **Either/or** | Oversimplifies by reducing to only two sides | "We can either stop using cars or destroy the earth." |
| **Ad hominem** | Attacks the person rather than their argument | "Green Peace's strategies aren't effective because they are all dirty hippies." |
| **Ad populum** | Appeals to what most people think to persuade | "If you were a true American you would support this." |
| **Red Herring** | Avoids the key issue by introducing an irrelevant one | "The level of mercury in seafood may be unsafe, but what will fishers do to support their families?" |
| **Straw Man** | Oversimplifies an opponent's view then attacks the weak version | "People who don't support the minimum wage hike hate the poor." |
| **Moral Equivalence** | Compares minor misdeeds with major atrocities | "That parking attendant who gave me a ticket is as bad as Hitler." |

## 12. Classical Argument Structure (Rhetorical Appeals + Components)

### The Four Rhetorical Appeals (Aristotle)

| Appeal | Definition | How to Build It |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | Persuasion through the author's credibility | Cite professional sources; use discipline-appropriate language; demonstrate expertise |
| **Logos** | Persuasion through logic | Present data, statistics, facts; sequence arguments clearly; draw explicit conclusions |
| **Pathos** | Persuasion through emotion or disposition | Story-telling, vivid imagery; appeal to reader's existing academic investment in the topic |
| **Kairos** | Persuasion through timing and urgency | Call to immediate action; frame the moment as opportune; establish why *now* matters |

**Usage note:** "One does not appeal to ethos, logos, or pathos directly. Rather, one appeals to an audience's emotion/disposition, reason/logic, or sense of the author's character/credibility within the text."

---

### Five Components of Classical Argument

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| **Exordium** | Introduction and hook — establish urgency (Kairos), gain reader interest, set tone |
| **Narratio** | Background and context — historical context, social perception, prior research |
| **Propositio + Partitio** | Thesis + brief roadmap — state your position and outline your argument (3–4 sentences max) |
| **Confirmatio + Refutatio** | Positive proof + counterargument response — prove your claims, then acknowledge and refute or concede opposing views |
| **Peroratio** | Conclusion — summarize findings, reassert claim, end on a compelling note; choose from: call to action, future implications, related research, historical parallels |

---

### On Counterarguments (Refutatio)
Two options:

**Concession:** Admit the opposing point has merit while showing why it doesn't defeat your argument. Use a framing phrase: "Despite all of the potential for positive learning..."

**Refutation:** Point out the flaws in the opposing argument directly.

---

## 13. Rhetorical Situation

A rhetorical situation is "any set of circumstances that involves at least one person using some sort of communication to modify the perspective of at least one other person."

Understanding the rhetorical situation helps writers create "strong, audience-focused, and organized writing." Ask before every section:
- Who is my reader?
- What do they already know?
- What do they need to be convinced of?
- What would make this moment persuasive for them?

## Quick Reference: Common Problems and Fixes

| Problem | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraph starts with a specific paper name instead of a claim | Missing topic sentence | Write the analytical point first; use the paper as evidence |
| Long sentence with multiple prepositions and "is/are" | Needs Paramedic Method | Apply the 7 steps |
| Multiple sentences in a row start with "The" | Sentence variety problem | Vary openings with dependent clauses, transitional phrases, participial phrases |
| Short choppy sentences | Needs subordination or conjunction | Combine with "because," "although," "which," etc. |
| "The construction community began to shift toward..." | Anthropomorphization + causal overclaim | Rewrite: "Studies from 2022–2024 increasingly employed..." |
| "critical," "profound," "paradigm-shifting" repeated | Word over-reliance | Replace with precise, varied language |
| Passive voice in the narrative analysis | Reduce for clarity | Apply Paramedic Method |
| Passive voice in methodology section | Correct for scientific writing | Apply Reverse Paramedic — keep or add passive |
| Technical term used without definition | Jargon without explanation | Define on first use; add brief parenthetical if mid-sentence |
| Claim stated without logical premises | Logic gap | Lay out: Premise → Evidence → Conclusion explicitly |
| Transition only connects adjacent sentences | Argument thread lost | Write transitions that signal the analytical direction, not just sequence |
