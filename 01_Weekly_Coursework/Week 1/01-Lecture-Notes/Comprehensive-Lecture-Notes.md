## COURSE OVERVIEW

### Course Purpose
This course teaches literature reviews as a **rigorous form of research** rather than simply a summary of previous studies. Over six weeks, students develop a substantial literature review manuscript on a topic relevant to their research interests.

### Five Core Review Methodologies
The course focuses on five major literature review methodologies:
1. **Narrative Review** - Traditional exploratory reviews
2. **Integrative Review** - Synthesizing diverse sources
3. **Theoretical Review** - Examining and developing theory
4. **Scoping Review** - Mapping research landscape
5. **Systematic Review** - Evidence-based decision making

### Key Principle: "Fit for Purpose"
**Central Concept:** The most appropriate methodology is the one that best aligns with your:
- Research purpose
- Review question
- Intended contribution

**Critical Understanding:** There is no universally "best" review type. Each approach serves a different purpose.

## FIVE LITERATURE REVIEW METHODOLOGIES

### 1. NARRATIVE REVIEW

#### Definition & Purpose
A traditional literature review that **summarizes and interprets** existing literature on a topic through flexible, descriptive synthesis.

#### Primary Characteristics
- **Flexibility:** Highly flexible and selective approach
- **Structure:** Low to moderate rigor
- **Search Process:** Flexible and selective; not formally documented
- **Transparency:** May lack transparency or rigor; potential for bias

#### When to Use
- **Best for:** Exploring a topic broadly
- **Research Questions:** Broad, exploratory questions
  - *Example: "What is known about this topic?"*
- **Use Case:** Early exploration, background understanding, literature overview

#### Strengths
- Flexible and accessible
- Useful for early exploration of topics
- Can provide comprehensive overview
- Allows author judgment in selection

#### Limitations
- May lack transparency or rigor
- Potential for bias in study selection
- Inconsistent methodology across articles
- Limited reproducibility

#### Search Strategy
- Flexible and selective
- Inclusion criteria often implicit or loosely defined
- May evolve as author explores literature

#### Data Sources
- Mixed: theoretical papers, empirical studies, reports, gray literature
- Not restricted to particular source types

#### Analysis Approach
- Thematic summary
- Narrative synthesis and interpretation
- Author-driven categorization

#### Typical Outcomes
- Broad overview and interpretation
- Narrative summary
- Foundational understanding

#### Common Publication Types
- Stand-alone review articles
- Introductory or conceptual articles
- Book chapters
- Background sections of dissertations/theses
- Position papers

#### What It Might Be Mistaken For
- Conceptual articles
- Overview chapters
- Unsystematic summaries

---

### 2. INTEGRATIVE REVIEW

#### Definition & Purpose
**Synthesizes literature across diverse sources** (both empirical and theoretical) to identify patterns, generate new insights, and develop conceptual frameworks.

#### Primary Characteristics
- **Combination:** Intentionally combines different methods and study types
- **Integration:** Brings together experimental and non-experimental research
- **Synthesis:** Creates new perspectives from diverse evidence
- **Structure:** Moderate rigor with structured but flexible methodology

#### When to Use
- **Best for:** Understanding patterns and trends across studies
- **Research Questions:** Comparative or pattern-seeking across diverse studies
  - *Example: "What patterns or insights emerge across studies?"*
- **Use Case:** When you need to synthesize diverse evidence types to generate new insights

#### Strengths
- Integrates diverse evidence effectively
- Generates new insights from existing research
- Develops conceptual frameworks
- Identifies underlying patterns

#### Limitations
- Can be inconsistent if poorly structured
- May resemble systematic reviews if methods are structured
- Requires careful synthesis methodology

#### Search Strategy
- Structured but flexible
- Inclusion criteria defined but may evolve
- Systematic and comprehensive but allows flexibility

#### Data Sources
- Primarily empirical + theoretical
- Combines different types of evidence
- May include both quantitative and qualitative studies

#### Analysis Approach
- Synthesis across studies
- Thematic analysis
- Framework development
- Pattern identification

#### Typical Outcomes
- New insights or conceptual frameworks
- Thematic synthesis
- Integrated understanding
- Framework or model development

#### Common Publication Types
- Stand-alone review articles
- Conceptual framework development papers
- Dissertation chapters
- Research agenda articles

#### What It Might Be Mistaken For
- Systematic reviews (especially if methods are highly structured)
- Narrative reviews (if synthesis is less rigorous)

---

### 3. THEORETICAL REVIEW

#### Definition & Purpose
**Examines, refines, or develops theory** by analyzing how concepts and theories have developed, identifying gaps, and advancing theoretical understanding.

#### Primary Characteristics
- **Focus:** Emphasis on concepts and frameworks
- **Purpose:** Theory development or clarification
- **Depth:** Deep conceptual understanding
- **Structure:** Moderate to high rigor

#### When to Use
- **Best for:** Refining or critiquing theory
- **Research Questions:** Questions about concepts and how theories develop
  - *Example: "How has a theory developed? What gaps exist?"*
- **Use Case:** When you need to advance theoretical understanding or develop a new framework

#### Strengths
- Deep conceptual understanding
- Advances theoretical knowledge
- Identifies conceptual gaps
- Develops new frameworks

#### Limitations
- May overlook empirical depth
- Requires strong theoretical foundation
- May be narrow in scope

#### Search Strategy
- Focused and concept-driven
- Inclusion criteria based on relevance to theory
- Selective and purposeful

#### Data Sources
- Conceptual papers and foundational texts
- Theoretical literature
- May include empirical studies if relevant to theory development

#### Analysis Approach
- Conceptual and theoretical analysis
- Framework development
- Critique and refinement of existing theories

#### Typical Outcomes
- Theory development or clarification
- Conceptual models
- Refined theoretical frameworks
- New theoretical perspectives

#### Common Publication Types
- Theory development articles
- Conceptual papers
- Scholarly essays
- Dissertation conceptual framework chapters

#### What It Might Be Mistaken For
- Narrative reviews
- Opinion/perspective articles

---

### 4. SCOPING REVIEW

#### Definition & Purpose
**Maps the extent and nature of literature** on a topic by identifying what research exists, what types of evidence are available, and what gaps exist in the literature.

#### Primary Characteristics
- **Goal:** Broad coverage of research landscape
- **Focus:** Identification of gaps and extent of literature
- **Structure:** Moderate rigor with systematic approach
- **Scope:** Comprehensive but less detailed synthesis

#### When to Use
- **Best for:** New or emerging topics; preliminary investigation
- **Research Questions:** Questions about what research exists on a topic
  - *Example: "What research exists on this topic?"*
- **Use Case:** When topic boundaries are unclear or you need initial understanding of research landscape

#### Strengths
- Identifies gaps in research
- Clarifies research landscape
- Maps extent of evidence available
- Useful for emerging topics

#### Limitations
- Less depth in synthesis compared to systematic reviews
- Descriptive rather than evaluative
- May not provide strong conclusions

#### Search Strategy
- Systematic and comprehensive
- Inclusion criteria broad and inclusive
- Aims for comprehensive coverage

#### Data Sources
- Wide range: empirical studies, gray literature, reports
- May include diverse source types
- Not restricted to published peer-reviewed studies

#### Analysis Approach
- Descriptive mapping and categorization
- Charting and organizing literature
- Identifying trends and patterns

#### Typical Outcomes
- Map or categorized overview of literature
- Identification of trends and gaps
- Visual representations (charts, tables)
- Preliminary understanding

#### Common Publication Types
- Stand-alone review articles
- Preliminary studies
- Research agenda papers
- Policy-oriented reports

#### What It Might Be Mistaken For
- Narrative reviews (due to broad scope)
- Systematic reviews (if methods appear rigorous)

#### Key Distinction from Integrative Review
**Scoping reviews** MAP THE RANGE of research available  
**Integrative reviews** IDENTIFY PATTERNS across studies

---

### 5. SYSTEMATIC REVIEW

#### Definition & Purpose
**Answers a focused research question** using a highly structured, transparent, and replicable methodology to evaluate evidence systematically.

#### Primary Characteristics
- **Structure:** Highly systematic and reproducible process
- **Rigor:** Highest rigor level
- **Transparency:** Fully documented and transparent methodology
- **Replicability:** Other researchers can replicate exact search

#### When to Use
- **Best for:** Evidence-based decision making with focused questions
- **Research Questions:** Specific, focused research questions
  - *Example: "What does the evidence show?" or "How does X impact Y?"*
- **Use Case:** When you need definitive evidence synthesis for decision-making

#### Strengths
- Transparent and reproducible
- Highly credible results
- Minimizes bias through structured processes
- Evidence-based conclusions

#### Limitations
- Time-intensive process
- Requires strict adherence to procedures
- Narrow focus may miss broader context
- Demands precise pre-planning

#### Search Strategy
- Highly systematic and reproducible
- Follows protocols (such as PRISMA)
- Predefined, explicit, and strict inclusion/exclusion criteria
- Documented and registered in advance

#### Data Sources
- Primarily empirical studies
- Specific criteria for study selection
- Varies by field and research question

#### Analysis Approach
- Structured synthesis (coding, comparison, meta-analysis if appropriate)
- Formal quality assessment
- Systematic data extraction

#### Typical Outcomes
- Evidence synthesis (tables, protocols, meta-analysis)
- Evidence-based conclusions
- Clear recommendations
- High-confidence findings

#### Common Publication Types
- Stand-alone evidence synthesis articles
- Health and social science reviews
- Policy reports
- Dissertation chapters
- Clinical practice guidelines

#### What It Might Be Mistaken For
- Narrative reviews
- Integrative reviews
- Scoping reviews

#### Requirements for Successful Systematic Reviews
1. **Narrow, specific research question**
2. **Clearly defined protocol** before search begins
3. **Transparent documentation** of all decisions
4. **Rigorous quality assessment**
5. **Replicable search strategy** with exact terms and databases
6. **Predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria**

## METHODOLOGY SELECTION FRAMEWORK

### Decision-Making Process

The selection of an appropriate review methodology should be guided by the **FIT FOR PURPOSE** principle:

#### Step 1: Define Your Research Purpose
- What do you want to accomplish with this literature review?
- What is your ultimate goal?
- How will the review be used?

#### Step 2: Develop Your Research Question
- Is your question **broad or narrow**?
- Do you need to **explore** or **answer definitively**?
- Is your topic **emerging** or **established**?

#### Step 3: Consider Your Intended Contribution
- Do you want to **discover patterns** across studies?
- Do you want to **develop or refine theory**?
- Do you want to **map the research landscape**?
- Do you want to **synthesize evidence** for decision-making?

#### Step 4: Evaluate Scope and Feasibility
- How much time do you have?
- What resources are available?
- What level of rigor is needed?

#### Step 5: Select Methodology
Match your purpose, question, and contribution to the methodology that best fits.

### Methodology Selection by Purpose

| **If Your Goal Is...** | **Select...** | **Because...** |
|---|---|---|
| Provide a broad overview of a topic | **Narrative** | Provides comprehensive, accessible summary |
| Identify patterns across diverse studies | **Integrative** | Synthesizes different evidence types and perspectives |
| Develop or refine a theory | **Theoretical** | Focuses on conceptual advancement |
| Map the research landscape | **Scoping** | Designed specifically for identifying what exists |
| Answer a specific question with evidence | **Systematic** | Provides most rigorous, replicable evidence synthesis |
| Explore an emerging topic | **Scoping** | Handles undefined topic boundaries |

### Methodology Selection by Research Question

| **Your Question Sounds Like...** | **Likely Methodology** |
|---|---|
| "What is known about this topic?" | Narrative Review |
| "What patterns emerge across studies?" | Integrative Review |
| "How has this theory developed?" | Theoretical Review |
| "What research exists on this topic?" | Scoping Review |
| "What does the evidence show?" | Systematic Review |

## CORE CONCEPTS AND KEY DEFINITIONS

### Literature Review as Research
A literature review is not merely a summary or background section. It is a **rigorous research methodology** that:
- Uses systematic processes
- Synthesizes existing knowledge
- Advances understanding
- Contributes original insights

### The "Fit for Purpose" Principle
The selection of methodology must align with:
1. **Your research purpose** - What you want to accomplish
2. **Your research question** - What you want to answer
3. **Your intended contribution** - What you want to add to the field

**Critical Rule:** Choose the methodology that BEST SERVES YOUR PURPOSE, not necessarily the "most rigorous" methodology.

### Important Conceptual Distinctions

#### Spectrum vs. Fixed Categories
**Key Understanding:** Review types exist on a **spectrum** rather than as fixed, isolated categories.

- Real-world articles may blend approaches
- Articles may only partially align with named methodologies
- When analyzing a review, prioritize:
  - The article's **purpose**
  - The **level of methodological structure**
  - **How** literature is selected and synthesized

#### Rigor vs. Appropriateness
- **Systematic reviews are the most rigorous**, but they are NOT always the best choice
- A **narrative review can be highly appropriate** for exploring an emerging topic
- A **theoretical review's "fit"** may be better than a rigorous systematic review for theory development

### How to Recognize Each Review Type

#### Look for Clues in Articles
When analyzing literature reviews, examine:

**In the Abstract/Introduction:**
- What is the stated purpose?
- What is the research question?
- What problem does the review address?

**In the Methods:**
- How explicit and structured is the search process described?
- What are the inclusion/exclusion criteria?
- Are search terms and databases specified?

**In the Results/Analysis:**
- How is the literature organized?
- Is data extracted and synthesized?
- Are there frameworks or models developed?

**In the Discussion:**
- What conclusions are drawn?
- What gaps are identified?
- What recommendations are made?

## WHEN TO USE EACH METHODOLOGY

### Decision Matrix

#### Use NARRATIVE REVIEW When:
✓ You are exploring a topic broadly  
✓ The topic is well-established with substantial literature  
✓ You want to provide accessible overview  
✓ You need flexibility in scope  
✓ You have limited time  
✓ Your goal is to orient readers to a topic area  

❌ Avoid if: You need definitive evidence, high credibility is essential, you must minimize bias

#### Use INTEGRATIVE REVIEW When:
✓ You need to synthesize diverse evidence types  
✓ You want to identify patterns across studies  
✓ Your goal is to develop new frameworks  
✓ You combine experimental and non-experimental research  
✓ You want to generate new insights  

❌ Avoid if: Topic is narrow and specific, high rigor is critical, or methodology must be highly transparent

#### Use THEORETICAL REVIEW When:
✓ You want to advance theoretical understanding  
✓ You're developing or refining concepts  
✓ Your focus is on how theory has evolved  
✓ You need to identify theoretical gaps  
✓ You're creating a conceptual framework  

❌ Avoid if: You need empirical evidence synthesis, your audience requires applied recommendations

#### Use SCOPING REVIEW When:
✓ You are examining a new or emerging topic  
✓ Topic boundaries are unclear  
✓ You want to map what research exists  
✓ You need to identify research gaps  
✓ You're doing preliminary investigation  
✓ Your topic may be too broad for systematic review  

❌ Avoid if: You need deep evaluation of study quality, specific evidence synthesis is required

#### Use SYSTEMATIC REVIEW When:
✓ You have a specific, focused research question  
✓ High credibility and rigor are essential  
✓ Results will inform policy or practice decisions  
✓ You must minimize bias  
✓ Replicability is important  
✓ You have sufficient time and resources  

❌ Avoid if: Your question is broad/exploratory, topic is emerging with limited literature, or time/resources are severely limited

## EVALUATION AND SELECTION CRITERIA

### Criteria for Evaluating Methodologies

#### 1. Research Question Clarity
| **Clarity Level** | **Indicator** | **Best Methodology** |
|---|---|---|
| Very broad | "What is known about X?" | Narrative or Scoping |
| Moderately broad | "What patterns exist in X?" | Integrative or Scoping |
| Focused on concepts | "How has concept X developed?" | Theoretical |
| Specific and narrow | "What is the effect of X on Y?" | Systematic |

#### 2. Topic Maturity
| **Topic Status** | **Characteristics** | **Best Methodology** |
|---|---|---|
| Emerging | New, limited literature, unclear boundaries | Scoping |
| Developing | Growing literature, some definition | Narrative or Scoping |
| Established | Substantial literature, clear concepts | Narrative, Integrative, or Theoretical |
| Well-defined | Extensive literature, specific focus | Systematic or Integrative |

#### 3. Available Resources

| **Resource Level** | **Time** | **Expertise** | **Best Methodology** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limited | < 3 months | Basic | Narrative or Scoping |
| Moderate | 3-6 months | Developing | Integrative or Theoretical |
| Substantial | 6-12+ months | Advanced | Systematic |

#### 4. Intended Use
| **Use** | **Requirements** | **Best Methodology** |
|---|---|---|
| Educational overview | Accessibility, clarity | Narrative |
| Framework development | Conceptual synthesis | Integrative or Theoretical |
| Policy/practice decision | High rigor, evidence-based | Systematic |
| Research agenda | Gap identification | Scoping |
| Theory advancement | Conceptual depth | Theoretical |

## COMMON MISTAKES AND MISMATCHES

### Critical Mistakes to Avoid

#### Mistake 1: Choosing Based on Rigor Alone
**❌ Wrong Thinking:** "Systematic reviews are most rigorous, so I should use that."  
**✓ Correct Thinking:** Choose the methodology that best serves your purpose. Rigor matters only if it fits your goal.

**Example:**  
If your goal is to **explore an emerging topic**, a systematic review would be **inappropriate** because:
- Topic boundaries are undefined
- Not enough literature may exist
- Research questions aren't specific enough

A **scoping review** would be better suited because it's designed for exactly this purpose.

#### Mistake 2: Mismatching Specificity
**❌ Wrong Thinking:** Using a systematic review for a broad, undefined question.  
**✓ Correct Thinking:** Systematic reviews require narrow, specific research questions.

**Example of Mismatch:**  
- **Question:** "What is known about educational technology?"  
- **Wrong Choice:** Systematic review (question too broad)  
- **Right Choice:** Narrative or Scoping review (better for broad questions)

#### Mistake 3: Confusing Purpose with Methodology
**❌ Wrong Thinking:** "I want to synthesize evidence, so I must use a systematic review."  
**✓ Correct Thinking:** Evidence synthesis can be achieved through multiple methodologies.

**Options for Evidence Synthesis:**
- **Integrative Review** - Synthesizes diverse evidence types
- **Systematic Review** - Synthesizes specific evidence systematically
- **Scoping Review** - Maps types of evidence available
- **Narrative Review** - Synthesizes literature narratively

#### Mistake 4: Ignoring the Spectrum
**❌ Wrong Thinking:** "This article is either a systematic review or it isn't."  
**✓ Correct Thinking:** Review types exist on a spectrum; real-world articles may blend approaches.

**Reality:**
- Articles may be primarily one type but include elements of others
- Hybrid approaches are acceptable when justified
- Methodological clarity matters more than rigid categorization

### Evaluation Checklist: Is There a Methodology Mismatch?

**Review your planned methodology against these questions:**

□ Does your research question fit the methodology's typical question type?  
□ Is your topic maturity appropriate for this methodology?  
□ Do you have adequate time and resources?  
□ Does the rigor level match your needs?  
□ Will this methodology generate what you hope to contribute?  
□ Are you choosing this because it's appropriate or because it's "better"?  

**If you answered "No" to any question, reconsider your methodology selection.**

### Red Flags: Signs of Potential Mismatch

🚩 **Broad, exploratory question + Systematic Review** = Mismatch  
🚩 **Narrow, specific question + Narrative Review** = Possible Mismatch  
🚩 **New/emerging topic + Systematic Review** = Likely Mismatch  
🚩 **Focused on theory + Systematic Review alone** = May need Theoretical Review  
🚩 **Need practical recommendations + Scoping Review** = Mismatch

## GUIDING PRINCIPLES FOR THIS COURSE

### Principle 1: Evidence Over Correctness
In analyzing reviews, the goal is **not** to guess the "correct" label but to:
- Use evidence from the article
- Explain your reasoning clearly
- Demonstrate understanding of concepts
- Support your classification with evidence

### Principle 2: Spectrum Thinking
Remember that:
- Review types exist on a spectrum
- Real articles may blend approaches
- Methodological hybrids are acceptable
- Context and purpose matter most

### Principle 3: Fit for Purpose
Always prioritize:
- Your research purpose
- Your research question
- Your intended contribution
- Over choosing the "best" or "most rigorous" methodology

### Principle 4: Transparency and Clarity
In your own literature review:
- Be explicit about your methodology choice
- Justify your selection based on fit
- Document your processes
- Explain your rationale

As you prepare your literature review, consider:

1. **Purpose:** What do I hope my literature review will accomplish?
2. **Question:** Can I articulate my research question clearly?
3. **Scope:** Is my topic broadly exploratory or narrowly focused?
4. **Maturity:** Is this an emerging or established topic?
5. **Contribution:** What do I want to add to the field?
6. **Audience:** Who will use this review and how?

## APPENDIX: COMMON QUESTIONS ANSWERED

### Q: Can a review be more than one type?
**A:** Yes! Real-world articles may blend approaches or partially align with multiple types. What matters is the **primary methodology** and how well the author justifies their approach.

### Q: Is systematic review always better than narrative?
**A:** No. A systematic review is highly rigorous but requires a narrow, specific question. For broad exploration, a narrative review is more appropriate and actually more valuable.

### Q: How do I decide between scoping and systematic?
**A:** 
- **Scoping:** When you want to know WHAT research exists (broad mapping)
- **Systematic:** When you want definitive answers to SPECIFIC questions

### Q: What if my topic seems to fit multiple methodologies?
**A:** That's normal! Select the one that best serves your PRIMARY purpose and research question.

### Q: Do I have to stick with one methodology throughout?
**A:** Yes. Your methodology should remain consistent with your research question and purpose.

### Q: What if my methodology changes as I do the review?
**A:** Document the change and explain why it was necessary. This is different from starting with the wrong methodology.
