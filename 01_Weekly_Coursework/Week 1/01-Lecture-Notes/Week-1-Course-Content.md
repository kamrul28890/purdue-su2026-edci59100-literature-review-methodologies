## Introduction to Literature-Based Research and Review Methodologies

In this course, you will learn how literature reviews can serve as a rigorous form of research rather than simply a summary of previous studies. Over the next six weeks, you will develop a substantial literature review manuscript on a topic relevant to your research interests.

These two videos introduce the five review methodologies we will examine in this course: narrative, integrative, theoretical, scoping, and systematic reviews and will get you thinking about which method you might use for your literature review.

### Video 1: Literature-Based Research and Major Review Methodologies

In this video provides an overview of the course and introduce the five major literature review methodologies. As you watch, focus on the purpose of each methodology and the kinds of questions it is designed to address.

After watching the first video, you should have a general understanding of the major review methodologies and how they differ. At this point, you are not expected to know which methodology you will use for your project.

### Video 2: Fit for Purpose

In this video explains the concept of fit for purpose. The most appropriate methodology is the one that best aligns with your research purpose, review question, and intended contribution.

There is no single "best" review methodology. Each approach serves a different purpose. The activities that follow will help you compare the methodologies and begin identifying which one may be the best fit for your literature review project.

## Methodology Comparison Guide

### Methodology Comparison

Understanding the similarities and differences among review methodologies will help you make an informed decision about which approach best fits your research purpose. This decision will guide the design of your literature review and shape the work you complete throughout the course.

You do not need to decide on your final methodology yet, but you should begin identifying one or two approaches that appear to be a strong fit for your topic and intended contribution.

#### Important Note

It is important to remember that review types exist on a spectrum rather than as fixed categories. While this chart highlights common characteristics, real-world articles may blend approaches or only partially align with a named methodology. When analyzing a review, prioritize:

- the purpose of the article
- the level of methodological structure
- how the literature is selected and synthesized

#### As You Review the Guide Below

Pay particular attention to the following questions:

- What is the primary purpose of each review methodology?
- What types of research questions are best suited to each approach?
- How structured and systematic is the search and selection process?
- What is the typical outcome or contribution of each methodology?
- What are the strengths and limitations of each approach?
- In what situations would each methodology be most appropriate?

#### Reflect on Your Own Project

As you read, begin considering:

- Which methodologies appear to align with your research interests?
- What do you hope your literature review will accomplish?
- Which methodology seems most appropriate for your topic and goals?
- What questions do you still have about the differences among the methodologies?

## Detailed Review Methodology Comparison Guide

| Criteria | Narrative Review | Integrative Review | Theoretical Review | Scoping Review | Systematic Review |
|----------|------------------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------|--------------------|
| **Primary Purpose** | Summarize and interpret existing literature | Synthesize literature across diverse sources (empirical + theoretical) | Examine, refine, or develop a theory | Map the extent and nature of literature | Answer a focused research question |
| **Key Features** | Flexible, descriptive | Combines different methods and study types | Focus on concepts and frameworks | Broad coverage, identifies gaps | Structured, transparent, replicable |
| **Best Use Case** | Exploring a topic broadly | Understanding patterns and trends | Refining or critiquing theory | New or emerging topics | Evidence-based decision making |
| **Types of Research Questions** | Broad, exploratory questions | Comparative or pattern-seeking across studies | Questions about concepts and theory | Questions about what research exists | Specific, focused research questions |
| **Typical Question** | What is known about this topic? | What patterns or insights emerge across studies? | How has a theory developed? What gaps exist? | What research exists on this topic? | What does the evidence show? |
| **Search Process** | Flexible and selective | Structured but flexible | Focused, concept-driven | Systematic and comprehensive | Highly systematic and reproducible |
| **Inclusion Criteria** | Often implicit or loosely defined | Defined but may evolve | Based on relevance to theory | Broad and inclusive | Predefined, explicit, strict |
| **Literature Data Sources** | Mixed (theoretical, empirical, reports) | Empirical + theoretical | Conceptual papers, foundational texts | Wide range (empirical, gray literature) | Primarily empirical studies (varies by field) |
| **Analysis Approach** | Thematic summary | Synthesis across studies | Conceptual and theoretical analysis | Descriptive mapping and categorization | Structured synthesis (e.g., coding, comparison) |
| **Primary Outcome** | Broad overview and interpretation | New insights or conceptual frameworks | Theory development or clarification | Identification of trends and gaps | Evidence-based conclusions |
| **Rigor Level** | Low–Moderate | Moderate | Moderate–High | Moderate | High |
| **Strengths** | Flexible, accessible, useful for early exploration | Integrates diverse evidence; generates new insights | Deep conceptual understanding; advances theory | Identifies gaps; clarifies research landscape | Transparent, reproducible, credible |
| **Limitations** | May lack transparency or rigor; potential bias | Can be inconsistent if poorly structured | May overlook empirical depth | Less depth in synthesis | Time-intensive; requires strict procedures |
| **Common Variations / Overlap** | May include systematic elements; sometimes labeled as "literature review" without specifying type | May resemble systematic reviews in search process; may include conceptual and empirical work together | — | May include extensive literature synthesis; sometimes framed as "review" without formal methodology | May resemble systematic reviews (if highly structured) or narrative reviews (if results are descriptive); often overlaps with other review types |
| **What it might be mistaken for** | Conceptual article, overview chapter, or unsystematic summary | Systematic review (especially if methods are structured) or narrative review | Narrative review or opinion/perspective article | Narrative review (due to broad scope) or systematic review (if methods appear rigorous) | Narrative review, integrative review, or scoping review |
| **Typical Output** | Narrative summary | Thematic synthesis | Conceptual model or framework | Map or categorized overview of literature | Evidence synthesis (may include tables, protocols) |
| **Typical Publication Types** | Stand-alone review articles; introductory or conceptual articles; book chapters; background sections of dissertations and theses; position papers | Stand-alone review articles; conceptual framework development papers; dissertation chapters; research agenda articles | Theory development articles; conceptual papers; scholarly essays; dissertation conceptual framework chapters | Stand-alone review articles; preliminary studies; research agenda papers; policy-oriented reports | Stand-alone evidence synthesis articles; health and social science reviews; policy reports; dissertation chapters; clinical practice guidelines |

Because this is an intensive six-week course, it is important to select a focused and feasible topic early so that you can devote your time to searching, analyzing, synthesizing, and writing rather than continuing to explore broad possibilities.

## Knowledge Check Activity: Methodology Comparison

### Purpose of This Activity

One of the most effective ways to learn the differences among literature review methodologies is to examine real examples and identify what makes each one distinct.

In this activity, you will act as a methodological "detective."

Using a small set of annotated examples, you will:
- Look for clues in each article
- Determine which review methodology the article most closely aligns with 
- Explain why that method fits the authors' purpose

The goal is to help you recognize the practical differences among review methodologies so that you can make a more informed decision about the approach that best fits your own project.

### What You Will Do

1. Review the provided annotated examples of published literature reviews:
   - Article 1
   - Article 2
   - Article 3
   - Article 4
   - Article 5

2. Keep the Detailed Review Literature Methodology Comparison Chart that you reviewed previously nearby to reference as needed

3. As you read each example, actively identify key features of the review and record your observations in the assignment document

4. Use the Literature Review Methodology Analysis Assignment document to:
   - Track differences across examples
   - Identify patterns
   - Compare methodologies
   - Respond to the reflection questions

You will use your observations and evidence to determine which review methodology each article most closely aligns with.

### What You Will Submit

Submit your completed Methodology Comparison Assignment document.

Your submission should include:
- Completed tables for each example article
- Your predicted review type and the actual review type (if provided)
- A brief explanation of what evidence supports your classification
- Your responses to the reflection questions

### How This Assignment Will Be Evaluated

This is a practice and learning activity, not a test of correctness. Think like a researcher: the objective is not to guess correctly. It is to use evidence to support your interpretation.

Therefore, your grade will not be based on whether you correctly identify every review type.

Instead, you will be evaluated on:
- Your effort and completeness
- Your use of evidence from the article
- Your ability to explain your reasoning

It is completely acceptable to make an incorrect prediction, as long as you explain your thinking clearly.
