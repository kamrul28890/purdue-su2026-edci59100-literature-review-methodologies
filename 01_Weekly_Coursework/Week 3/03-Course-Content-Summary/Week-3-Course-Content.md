2B. Build 2–3 Search Strings
💡 Why use search strings?
Academic databases do not understand full questions; they match specific words and combinations of words.
This means:

If your search is too simple → too many unrelated results
If your search is too narrow → you may miss important studies
👉 Search strings help you control and refine your results.

👉 How to build a search string:

Identify 2–3 key concepts from your research question
List synonyms for each concept
Combine them using:
AND → connects different concepts
OR → connects similar terms
Small changes in wording will produce different results. You are testing. You are  not finalizing.
Example Search Strings:

("artificial intelligence" OR AI) AND (feedback OR "automated feedback") AND writing

AI AND writing AND ("learning outcomes" OR performance)
🔹 How Databases Read Your Search
Databases match exact words and combinations—not natural language.

Boolean operators must be in ALL CAPS
Quotation marks keep phrases together
Parentheses control grouping
Small changes in formatting can significantly change results.

Narrative Review. Accordion 1 of 5.
For a narrative review:

You are not expected to find every study on your topic.
Instead, focus on identifying key and relevant studies that help explain the topic.
Searching Guidance:
You may begin with broader searches but refine your results to focus on studies that are most relevant and influential for explaining your topic.

Other Places to Search:

Citation chaining (also called citation analysis or citation tracing) is one of the most effective ways to find relevant literature 

Backward Snowballing: Look at the bibliography/reference list of the most relevant "seed" articles you've already found to discover foundational studies
Forward Snowballing: Look up your seed articles on Google Scholar and click the "Cited by" link to find newer research that has built upon those papers. Scopus and Web of Science also offer this.
Documentation:
Narrative reviews do not follow rigid, exhaustive search protocols. Therefore, you do not need to document every search or result, but you should be able to explain how you identified the key studies included in your review.

You Should Prioritize Including in Your Paper:

Foundational works
Recent research

If your topic is AI in language learning, you might include:

Foundational studies

Influential perspectives

Advanced Search Techniques:

To build a solid theoretical framework, avoid simple keyword searches. Use these techniques to narrow your focus:
Phrase Searching: Use quotation marks around concepts (e.g., "cognitive dissonance") to keep terms together.
Boolean Operators: Combine different facets of your theory using $AND$ and $OR$. For example: (theory OR model) AND ("knowledge sharing" OR "knowledge transfer").
Proximity Searching: Use tools like $w/n$ (e.g., theory w/5 leadership) to find articles with terms appearing within a specific distance of each other.
Filter by Document Type: Filter your results specifically to Review or Theoretical Paper to isolate high-level conceptual discussions from purely empirical, data-driven studies.
Documentation:
You do not need to document all searches, but you should be able to explain how the selected works represent the development of the theory or concept.

👉 Think: “What works help me understand how this idea or theory has developed?”

The goal of a scoping review search is to retrieve all results that are relevant to your topic in order to map (i.e., report on the scope), and to identify themes and gaps in the literature. Therefore, you are expected to capture the range of research that exists, not just a few key studies.

When searching for literature for a scoping review, you should conduct a broad and comprehensive search across as many databases, journals, conference papers, and other source locations as possible. You should also search for "Grey Literature". Scoping reviews are unique in that they mandate exploring unpublished or non-commercial literature to capture the full scope of a topic. 

You might include:

Different populations
Different contexts
Different study types
Searching Guidance:
Use multiple databases, search terms, and source types to ensure broad coverage. You may need to iterate and expand your search to capture the full range of literature.

Search Updates

Almost as soon as a scoping review search is completed, the results start becoming outdated. Because of this, some journals require these types of review searches to have been run within six months to a year of publication.

When you are nearing submission to a journal, to perform a search update.

Documentation:
You should document your search process to show how you captured a wide range of studies across contexts, populations, and study types.

To meet PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines, you must document:
Exact search strings for each database, including dates of searches.
The number of results found per database.
The flow of records (Identification, Screening, and Inclusion) to be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram
Pro Tip: Always save and export your raw search result files (e.g., .ris files or search history logs) with the date stamp in case peer reviewers or future researchers request the original data. 

👉 Your goal is to show what has been studied and what has not.

Systematic Review. Accordion 5 of 5.

If you are conducting a Systematic Review:

You must follow a clearly defined and documented process. You will need to include an explanation of your process in your paper.

You should:

Use defined search terms in advance
Search multiple databases
Use clear inclusion/exclusion criteria
Record how many articles you found and excluded
Record why excluded any articles
Remember - Your process should be transparent and replicable. 

Searching Guidance:
Your search strategy should be planned in advance and applied consistently across databases using clearly defined search strings. You might also search beyond traditional journals to avoid publication bias for "Grey Literature". This includes clinical trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), conference abstracts, and regulatory documents.

Search Updates

Almost as soon as a scoping review search is completed, the results start becoming outdated. Because of this, some journals require these types of review searches to have been run within six months to a year of publication.

When you are nearing submission to a journal, to perform a search update.

Documentation:
You must fully document your search process, including search strings, databases used, number of results, and reasons for inclusion or exclusion.

To meet PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, you must document:
Database Details: Name of the database/platform, the interface (e.g., Ovid, EBSCO, PubMed), and the date parameters
Full Search Strings: Copy and paste the exact, unedited search code used for every single database. Including dates of searches.
Search Modifications: Note any modifications or syntax translations made when adapting your search from one database to another. 
Limits & Filters: Explicitly list any applied limits, such as language, publication year, or human/animal tags. 
Searcher Information: Disclose if a professional librarian or information specialist helped design or execute the search. 
Results: The number of results found from databases, registers, and other sources (e.g., citation searching). 
Screening: Number of records removed before screening (e.g., duplicates), and the number of records screened vs. excluded. 
Included: Number of full-text articles assessed for eligibility, the reasons for full-text exclusion, and the final number of included studies/reports. 
The items above, decisions and flow of records (Identification, Screening, and Inclusion) must be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram in your paper.

Pro Tip: Always save and export your raw search result files (e.g., .ris files or search history logs) with the date stamp in case peer reviewers or future researchers request the original data.
