IntroductionConstruction job sites are dynamic environments involving various workers, equipment, and machines operating simultaneously, resulting in managing such job sites a complex and challenging task (Alaloul et al. 2022). Compared with some other engineering sectors such as automotive and aerospace, construction is heavily labor intensive, has a vast and diverse supply chain, and is dominated heavily by single point and ad-hoc solutions, which results in considering every project is different (Davila Delgado and Oyedele 2021). Because construction sites have these characteristics, standard industrial monitoring systems are often not practical for use in the construction industry (Alaloul et al. 2022).Due to this profound complexity, regular safety inspections are needed to keep the work environment hazard-free (Asadzadeh et al. 2020). Additionally, managers must monitor and track activities and ensure the project meets the expected production rates (Alaloul et al. 2022). However, traditional manual observation and reporting methods, such as physical site walk-throughs for hazard identification or the manual logging of Daily Construction Reports (DCRs) heavily rely on subjective and error-prone manual visual inspection procedures (Samsami 2024). Furthermore, these manual methods are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and result in a slow information flow (Alaloul et al. 2022).The absence of real-time information resulting from these manual limitations hinders monitoring schedule, cost, and other performance indicators, reducing the ability to reveal or manage the variability and uncertainty inherent in project activities (Alaloul et al. 2022). Because of these systemic bottlenecks, the industry persistently struggles to overcome the issues associated with safety hazards and low productivity (Jiang and Messner 2023). Consequently, there is a growing interest in the construction industry in adopting AI technologies for various management and construction tasks (Rabbi and Jeelani 2024), with computer vision emerging as a prominent solution to capture and analyze data from these complex sites (Paneru and Jeelani 2021).To overcome the data deficiencies of traditional manual methods, research initially shifted toward automated object-level data collection (Tuomisto et al. 2026). Rather than broadly applying automation to all construction tasks, this specific technological domain focused on developing automated, real-time performance monitoring systems consisting of location tracking, activity recognition, and performance monitoring (Sherafat et al. 2020). To achieve this, the first of these methods involved attaching tracking devices like Radio Frequency Identification (RFID), Ultra-Wideband (UWB), or Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to building components (Tuomisto et al. 2026). However, while these tag-based methods are effective for locating materials, they cannot verify the proper installation or condition of components, limiting their utility for detailed inspection tasks (Tuomisto et al. 2026). Furthermore, tracking eventually thousands of items do not offer an economical or practical approach in large-scale projects (Bohn and Teizer 2010; Chen et al. 2023). Eventually, the industry shifted toward computer vision techniques to address this problem, as they operate without requirements to attach any sensors and enable more comprehensive on-site information, such as the locations and activities of construction entities, bringing operational and technical advantages over various wearable sensors (Xiong et al. 2019).Building upon the shift toward camera-based monitoring, traditional computer vision approaches utilizing bounding-box detectors have been widely adopted to locate construction entities. While these models are highly effective at spatial localization, identifying only the workers and PPE components, without recognizing the contextual relationship between them, does not effectively verify PPE compliance (Nath, Behzadan, and Paal 2020). Specifically, inferring that workers are prone to experience falling from height based on safety regulations is difficult for computer vision; this condition can be summarized as a semantic gap (Wu et al. 2021). Because computer vision cannot natively comprehend safety rulebooks, traditional pipelines attempted to bridge this gap by applying an algorithm with explicit rules that are determined through empirical observations (Nath et al. 2020). However, manually establishing these rules for multiple PPEs is a complicated task and results may not be structured or comprehensive enough to consider all possible scenarios (Nath et al. 2020).To overcome the limitation of manual geometric mapping, research explored an alternative approach using Natural Language Processing (NLP) for automatically extracting regulatory requirements from textual regulatory documents and formalizing these requirements in a logic format (Zhang and El-Gohary 2015). Yet, while NLP excels at rule extraction, these text-based models are fundamentally blind to the physical site. Ultimately, they simply represent the extracted safety requirements in the form of query graphs for supporting subsequent knowledge graph-based field compliance checking (Wang and El-Gohary 2023). Consequently, this creates a profound technological disconnect that computer vision can see the site but cannot understand the rules, while NLP understands the rules but cannot see the site. This fundamental limitation necessitates the emergence of multimodal Vision-Language Models (VLMs) capable of natively fusing both data streams.To address the fundamental limitations of traditional methods in processing semantic information, recent advancements have introduced multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) upon which vision-language models (VLMs) combine image processing and natural language processing (NLP) (Chan et al. 2025). By extracting both intricate visual and linguistic features, these models can perform multiple types of instruction-following tasks such as image captioning, visual grounding, and visual question answering (VQA) (Chan et al. 2025). Furthermore, these architectures acquire generalized visual representations guided by natural language supervision, enabling impressive zero-shot detection capabilities (Zhang et al. 2025). By leveraging natural language prompts and contextual reasoning, VLMs offer a flexible approach for spatiotemporal analysis interpreting both the spatial geometry of hazards and the temporal sequence of complex human behaviors on dynamic construction sites, potentially reducing the dependence on extensive labeled datasets (Bui, Chodosh, and Tavakoli 2026). Consequently, these multimodal systems can create human-readable captions for daily progress and work activity logs (Jung et al. 2024), while simultaneously supporting interactive question-and-answer sessions, fostering a more informed understanding of construction site safety (Hussain et al. 2026).Despite the rapid emergence of VLMs as a paradigm-shifting technology, existing systematic reviews have remained siloed in their respective unimodal domains. The majority of existing reviews on AI applications in the construction industry tend to focus on specific domains within AI, such as computer vision or NLP, thereby addressing only fragments of the potential AI applications (Rabbi and Jeelani 2024). For instance, previous reviews have extensively mapped pure computer vision research in construction (Jiang and Messner 2023; Paneru and Jeelani 2021; Zhong et al. 2019) or provided overviews of sensor-based safety management (Asadzadeh et al. 2020). While these studies provide valuable baselines, existing reviews do not cover the latest technological advances and lack systematic elaboration for construction scene understanding (Zhang et al. 2025). Specifically, although Vision-Language Models (VLMs) excel in general multimodal tasks, their ability to understand human behavior in safety-critical construction environments remains largely unexplored (Bui et al. 2026). Due to the development of scene understanding and large language models in recent years, it is necessary to conduct a new review on this topic to capture these paradigm-shifting advancements (Zhang et al. 2025).To address this critical gap, this paper presents a comprehensive scoping review of multimodal vision-language systems applied to construction safety and progress monitoring. Conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines (Tricco et al. 2018), the specific objectives of this study are to: (1) map the current landscape of VLM applications in construction management; (2) develop a robust taxonomy bridging foundational VLM architectures with practical construction management applications; and (3) analyze existing algorithmic limitations to outline a roadmap for future research in construction. To achieve these objectives, this review seeks to explicitly answer the following four primary research questions (RQs):RQ1: What are the prevalent Vision-Language Model (VLM) architectures currently utilized for spatiotemporal analysis in construction?RQ2: How is multimodal data (visual and textual) fused and processed to evaluate safety hazards on active sites?RQ3: What are the primary methodological bottlenecks in deploying these models for real-time progress tracking and safety?RQ4: How is the performance of Vision-Language Models currently evaluated and benchmarked for spatiotemporal tasks in construction?Research MethodologyThis review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines (Tricco et al. 2018). A scoping review design was deliberately selected over traditional quantitative meta-analysis for two primary reasons. First, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have emerged as a promising tool for visual understanding tasks (Bui et al. 2026), and recent studies are widely investigating these multi-modal feature mining pipelines to shed light on novel opportunities in the construction industry (Chan et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024); thus, the primary objective is to map the extent, range, and nature of this novel architecture rather than to compare narrow intervention effects. Second, the evaluation metrics across the included studies exhibit substantial methodological heterogeneity; ranging from geometric precision metrics, such as mean Average Precision (Nath et al. 2020; Wang and El-Gohary 2024), to natural language generation metrics, such as BLEU and CIDEr (Jung et al. 2024; Xiao, Wang, and Kang 2022), which renders a standardized statistical meta-analysis inappropriate.2.1 Search Strategy and Information SourcesTo ensure a rigorous and reproducible retrieval process, the literature search was conducted on 4th March 2026 across two of the most comprehensive scientific databases for engineering and computer science research: the Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection and Scopus. These specific databases were selected because the WoS contains the most comprehensive and influential journals within the field of construction and engineering management (Zhong et al. 2019), and Scopus is actively used alongside WoS as a primary search engine for AI applications in the construction domain (Rabbi and Jeelani 2024). The keywords were determined by analyzing frequent words found in papers where AI was applied to enhance construction management and safety (Rabbi and Jeelani 2024). The search query was constructed using a tripartite Boolean logic framework designed to intersect three distinct conceptual domains: (1) the specific AI architecture (Multimodal vision-language systems), (2) the analytical task (spatiotemporal and dynamic reasoning), and (3) the application domain (construction management). While previous scientometric mappings focused more on the contributing authors and geographic origins of the literature, the current study emphasizes the specific technical applications and methodological mechanisms of computer vision research in construction. Compiling literature in this manner enables the identification of issues, opportunities and challenges that are not currently well covered and examined. Yet, there is a need to develop a framework of the CV approaches that can be applied to construction use cases to inform future research and development activities. To systematically structure this review and address this need, the following sections propose a Three-Layered Spatiotemporal VLM Framework. By exploring the chronological evolution of these algorithms (Layer 2: The Engine Layer), this chapter explicitly addresses RQ1 (prevalent VLM architectures) and RQ2 (multimodal data fusion) by demonstrating how raw visual and textual inputs (Layer 1: Multimodal Inputs) are mathematically fused. Ultimately, mapping the engine layer establishes the seamless foundation for analyzing their practical construction applications in Chapter 5 (Layer 3: The Output Layer).4.1 The Three-Layered Spatiotemporal VLM Framework To systematically map the rapid technological shifts in construction AI, the proposed Three-Layered Spatiotemporal VLM Framework was established to capture the field's chronological progression. The structural decision to constrain this framework to precisely three layers is not arbitrary; rather, it is fundamentally grounded in the established continuum of human information processing for automated systems. In automated environments, decisions are derived from functions that replicate human cognition, categorized into three primary hardware and software mechanisms: sensors, processors, and actuators (Czarnowski et al., 2020; Parasuraman et al., 2000).The architecture of this framework maps directly to these three interdependent cognitive stages. Layer 1 functions as the "sensory" acquisition stage, consisting of multimodal inputs where raw visual data streams are captured alongside textual domain knowledge and kinematic sensor data. Layer 2 represents the Spatiotemporal VLM Core, acting as the "processor" that handles perception and decision-making by mimicking human cognitive functions through algorithmic design (Czarnowski et al., 2020). This engine layer is critically necessary since approaches for representing linguistic information of regulatory rules using graph structure have not yet emerged, given that the pairwise relationships in traditional scene graphs cannot represent the complex relationships in regulatory rules (Chen et al., 2022). Consequently, Layer 2 categorizes the algorithmic solutions into three chronological paradigms: Zero/Few-Shot Prompting, Domain Fine-Tuned VLMs, and Hybrid Geometric-Semantic Systems. Finally, Layer 3 serves as the "actuator" stage, translating these cognitive engine processes into actionable physical outputs and construction management tasks, divided primarily into safety management and progress tracking.This tripartite structure aligns perfectly with the required progression of construction scene understanding, illustrating the evolution from rule-based learning to end-to-end learning by categorizing the flow of data from raw inputs to high-level semantics, and ultimately to automated interventions (Li et al., 2025). Crucially, this framework explicitly emphasizes spatiotemporal analysis due to the highly dynamic nature of construction job sites, requiring models to track entities across both physical space and continuous time. For instance, detecting moving objects in construction sites and tracking equipment cycles necessitates temporal video analysis rather than static image processing. By fusing spatial geometry with temporal sequence tracking, these models offer a comprehensive overview of existing research for the purpose of identifying gaps in the body of knowledge and anticipating future research directions. To understand the operational mechanics of this spatiotemporal engine, it is necessary to first examine the earliest applications of foundation models that attempted to extract information independent of task-specific retraining./4.2 Paradigm A: Zero/Few-Shot Prompting (Retrieval-Oriented) The deployment of traditional computer vision on construction sites has historically been bottlenecked by the need for extensive annotated datasets. Because traditional supervised learning methods suffer from high annotation costs, severe computational demands, and limited datasets, scaling these systems to cover every potential hazard or piece of equipment has proven prohibitively labor-intensive (Zhang et al., 2025). To circumvent this strict reliance on massive data collection, the construction research community began seeking training-free alternatives. This search led to the adoption of foundational Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP), DINOv2, and Florence-2. Rather than relying on rigid numerical labels defined during a lengthy training phase, these multimodal methods acquire generalized visual representations guided by natural language supervision, enabling impressive zero-shot detection capabilities without requiring task-specific retraining (Zhang et al., 2025).The operational mechanics of this retrieval-oriented paradigm rely on embedding both visual and textual inputs into a shared semantic space, effectively allowing language to guide visual perception. For instance, contrastive pre-training enables the model to train an unseen class using only its description in the text (Gil & Lee, 2024). By transforming class names into descriptive text prompts, the cosine similarity between query visual features and N text prompts from the target dataset is measured to classify the objects (Liang et al., 2024). This shared embedding space completely alters how algorithms process site data. State-of-the-art models like Florence-2 are capable of open vocabulary object detection, meaning they can detect objects described by a text prompt without being limited to predefined classes (Jeoung et al., 2025). By simply altering the textual query, researchers have successfully deployed this retrieval paradigm to recognize temporary construction site objects (Liang et al., 2024), track the tasks of heavy earthmoving equipment (Jeoung et al., 2025), and verify personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance through semantic text similarity (Gil & Lee, 2024).However, while zero-shot and few-shot prompting successfully mitigates data scarcity by extracting bounding boxes and object tags, this paradigm suffers from a fundamental limitation in semantic expressiveness. Because current VLMs are trained on broad, general-purpose datasets, they lack optimization for task-specific applications like construction site monitoring (Bui et al., 2026). Specifically, these foundational models cannot deal with domain-specific knowledge directly because they are pre-trained on general objects and remain unfamiliar with complex architectural and engineering technical terms (Gil & Lee, 2024). More critically, this retrieval paradigm merely outputs discrete object locations and isolated labels. It fundamentally lacks the capacity to generate coherent, human-readable narratives that describe the continuous interactions and ongoing activities within an active scene. To address this descriptive gap and translate visual data into comprehensive situational awareness, researchers transitioned toward generative adaptation, marking the critical evolutionary shift to Paradigm B.4.3 Paradigm C: Hybrid Geometric-Semantic Systems and Ontologies To overcome the descriptive gap left by zero-shot retrieval models, the construction research community recognized that outputting isolated object tags was insufficient for comprehensive site monitoring. Consequently, the industry transitioned toward generative adaptation by adopting image captioning algorithms. Unlike traditional object detection that merely outputs discrete labels, image captioning combines computer vision and natural language processing to translate visual information into comprehensive natural language descriptions (Liu et al., 2020). The operational mechanics of this paradigm typically rely on an encoder-decoder framework, where a visual encoder (such as a CNN) extracts image embedding features, and a language decoder (such as a recurrent neural network) generates the corresponding text sequences (Xiao et al., 2022). By training these generative architectures directly on custom construction datasets, the models successfully acquire domain-specific vocabulary and can manifest complex construction activity scenes into human-readable narratives (Liu et al., 2020).This ability to generate semantically rich sentences completely transformed automated construction management, particularly in automating tedious administrative and safety documentation. For safety management, integrating deep learning object detection with image captioning enabled safety systems to generate rich text with semantic information to proactively identify hazards (Wang et al., 2024). In the domain of progress tracking, this paradigm enabled the automated generation of daily logs. For instance, recent architectures like VisualSiteDiary utilize a detector-free Vision-Language Transformer model for captioning photologs to support daily construction reporting (Jung et al., 2024). By fine-tuning the transformer on diverse site images, the system successfully extracts actionable insights around the "where," "how," and "what" of the work that happened on-site on a particular calendar date (Jung et al., 2024).However, despite these remarkable advances in scene description, domain fine-tuned captioning suffers from a severe interactive limitation. While generative fine-tuning successfully creates descriptive sentences, it remains a strictly one-way, static process. Existing deep learning applications in this paradigm lack the capability to provide context-specific and interactive feedback that is essential for workers to fully understand their surrounding environments (Chen et al., 2024). Because a captioning model generates a fixed narrative and stops, site managers cannot ask it follow-up questions, query it about specific unmentioned hazards, or ask it to dynamically cross-reference its visual findings against complex, multi-step safety rulebooks. To resolve these limitations in user interactivity and facilitate dynamic querying, recent methodologies have advanced beyond static captioning toward the deployment of agentic Large Multimodal Models (MLLMs), marking the critical evolutionary shift to Paradigm C.4.4 Paradigm C: Hybrid Geometric-Semantic Systems (Agentic & Hybrid Stacks)To overcome the interactive bottleneck of static image captioning, the construction research community shifted toward agentic Large Multimodal Models (MLLMs). While previous paradigms successfully described scenes, they struggled to correlate visual observations directly with complex regulatory codes due to inherent limitations in geometric reasoning (Hussain et al., 2026). To bridge this gap, Paradigm C introduces hybrid frameworks that combine precise geometric reasoning (such as bounding box coordinates or Building Information Modeling data) with LLM-powered interpretation to achieve true, regulation-aware scene understanding (Hussain et al., 2026).The operational mechanics of these hybrid systems rely on fusing object detection arrays with conversational AI and explicit domain ontologies. For instance, recent intelligent assistant systems utilize YOLO architectures to extract the precise geometric coordinates of workers and equipment, and then feed those detections directly into an MLLM as text prompts (Hussain et al., 2026). This allows an interactive chatbot within the application to present the generated safety caption to the user while allowing for further discussion, questions, or additional information in a dynamic, two-way dialogue (Hussain et al., 2026). To ensure these MLLMs do not hallucinate and strictly adhere to industry standards, researchers have developed hierarchical prompting frameworks driven by a construction safety ontology, which explicitly embeds construction-related knowledge into the model as part of the prompts (Chan et al., 2025). By operating as a human-alike virtual assistant agent, these multi-modal models can perform complex visual question answering (VQA) to interactively check compliance, such as confirming if a worker is maintaining three-point contact or attached to a secure anchorage point (Chan et al., 2025).This hybrid, agentic paradigm has rapidly expanded the boundaries of automated construction management across both time and physical space. In the temporal domain, MLLMs are now being deployed to process multiple consecutive video frames, enabling complex spatial inference, task inference, and temporal event analysis to monitor the continuous operational tasks of heavy equipment over time (Jeoung et al., 2025). In the spatial domain, these vision-language models have been integrated directly with quadruped robots and Building Information Modeling (BIM) (Tuomisto et al., 2026). As the robot autonomously navigates a site, the VLM actively compares captured images against metadata extracted from the IFC (Industry Foundation Classes) model to deliver programmatic assessments on whether specific architectural elements are actually present and installed correctly (Tuomisto et al., 2026).However, while Paradigm C represents the absolute cutting edge of spatiotemporal construction scene understanding, it is not without critical vulnerabilities. The literature explicitly notes that despite significant progress in compliance checking, these advanced models are still challenged by dynamic scenarios and complex behavioral detection (Hussain et al., 2026). Furthermore, their reliance on complex prompt engineering and their sensitivity to environmental noise introduces new risks of latency and unreliability in safety-critical settings.Chapter 5: Thematic Analysis – Construction Management Applications This chapter investigates how the cognitive processes of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are translated into actionable construction management deliverables. Within the built environment, the high-level semantic outputs generated by these models—such as captions, Visual Question Answering (VQA) responses, and compliance reports—are predominantly deployed across two critical operational domains. Section 5.1 explores the application of these models in Safety and Risk Management, tracing the progression from basic compliance checks to interactive hazard querying. Subsequently, Section 5.2 examines Progress Tracking and Automated Reporting, detailing how multimodal AI automates site logging and complex equipment tracking workflows.5.1 Domain A: Construction Safety and Risk ManagementHistorically, the integration of computer vision into safety management began by targeting the most visible and easily preventable hazards, specifically the absence of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE). Utilizing Paradigm A zero-shot retrieval architectures, early automated frameworks verified PPE compliance without requiring task-specific training. By extracting human skeletal key points, these systems cropped individual body-part images and compared generated image captions against natural language prompts describing OSHA safety regulations, determining PPE compliance through cosine similarity (Gil & Lee, 2024). However, while identifying whether a worker is wearing a hardhat is a critical first step, safety incidents often arise from complex spatiotemporal interactions rather than mere equipment absence. While identifying the presence of PPE is a foundational requirement, comprehensive risk management must also evaluate complex spatiotemporal interactions, as workers may be fully equipped yet still engage in high-risk operational behaviors.To comprehensively capture these risky interactions, the industry shifted toward Paradigm B, utilizing domain fine-tuned generative models to analyze unsafe behaviors. By integrating deep learning object detection with image captioning, safety systems gained the capability to extract semantic information and generate rich textual descriptions of active scenes, enabling the proactive identification of hazards by matching these visual descriptions against text-based safety rules (Wang et al., 2024). Yet, despite successfully generating descriptive safety narratives, these systems remained static and lacked context-specific, interactive feedback essential for workers to fully comprehend their surrounding environments (Chen et al., 2024).To break through this interactive bottleneck, safety monitoring evolved into Paradigm C, deploying hybrid agentic Large Multimodal Models (MLLMs) capable of complex reasoning and dialogue. For instance, the Visual Construction Safety Query (VCSQ) system integrates real-time image captioning and safety-centric VQA with head-mounted Augmented Reality (AR) devices, allowing workers to actively query the system about surrounding hazards on-demand (Chen et al., 2024). Furthermore, to ensure these agentic models do not hallucinate and strictly adhere to regulatory codes, researchers have developed human-alike virtual assistants driven by explicit construction safety ontologies. By injecting specific domain knowledge into the model's prompting framework, these systems can perform complex VQA to check multi-step compliance, such as verifying work-at-height regulations (Chan et al., 2025). Similarly, mobile-based intelligent assistants now combine precise geometric reasoning from object detectors with LLM-powered interpretation to generate comprehensive hazard captions and facilitate interactive safety discussions with site inspectors (Hussain et al., 2026).However, despite these profound advancements in identifying physical hazards and compliance violations, current general-purpose VLMs still struggle to interpret deeply nuanced visual cues, such as distinguishing whether a crew is effectively collaborating or simply communicating (Bui et al., 2026). Furthermore, mitigating safety risks represents only one half of the site management equation. The exact same spatiotemporal engines that evaluate hazards are simultaneously required to monitor site productivity, effectively forcing the industry to adapt these multimodal systems for progress tracking.5.2 Domain B: Progress Tracking and Automated ReportingWhile ensuring worker safety remains the paramount priority of site management, mitigating risks represents only half of the operational equation. The exact same spatiotemporal engines deployed to identify hazards are simultaneously utilized to monitor site productivity and automate administrative documentation. The foundation of automated progress tracking requires a fundamental understanding of where site resources are located. Historically, locating these assets required massive annotation efforts, but researchers have successfully adapted training-free, zero-shot architectures to circumvent this bottleneck. By leveraging an ImageNet-based similarity cache alongside contrastive language-image pre-training, algorithms can accurately recognize temporary on-site objects by comparing their visual features against open-source text labels without task-specific retraining (Liang et al., 2024). Similarly, training-free multimodal frameworks now fuse object detectors with foundational models to extract localized bounding boxes for specific construction tools and materials (Zhang et al., 2025).However, simply locating a tool or a pile of materials does not explain the broader context of the work being performed. To automate the tedious administrative burden of daily site logging, the industry shifted toward generative adaptation. Moving beyond isolated bounding boxes, generative models translate visual workflows into comprehensive text. For instance, architectures such as VisualSiteDiary utilize detector-free Vision-Language Transformers to automatically caption site photologs (Jung et al., 2024). By fine-tuning the model on diverse site images, the system successfully extracts actionable insights regarding the "where," "how," and "what" of the work that occurred on-site on a specific calendar date to support the generation of Daily Construction Reports (DCRs) (Jung et al., 2024).Yet, construction progress is inherently a continuous, dynamic process rather than a static daily snapshot. To truly understand the ongoing operational state of heavy machinery, the field evolved into Paradigm C, deploying agentic Large Multimodal Models (MLLMs) to process sequential video frames over time. These advanced zero-shot frameworks can evaluate complex multiple-choice questions regarding spatial inference, task inference, and temporal events to determine exactly what actions an excavator is performing, or exactly which frames show a dump truck sitting idle (Jeoung et al., 2025).A prominent advancement in automated progress tracking involves the integration of conversational MLLMs directly with physical robotics and Building Information Modeling (BIM) data. In cutting-edge automated inspection frameworks, quadruped robots autonomously navigate partially built environments (Tuomisto et al., 2026). As the robot captures images, an integrated foundational vision-language model acts as a virtual inspector, analyzing the visual data against metadata extracted directly from the Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) model. This hybrid geometric-semantic system allows the VLM to deliver programmatic, binary assessments on whether specific architectural elements are physically present and installed correctly, fundamentally bridging the gap between the digital twin and physical reality (Tuomisto et al., 2026).Table X.
